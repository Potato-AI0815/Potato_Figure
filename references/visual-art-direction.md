# Visual art direction

## Evidence architecture first

For each figure, write:

```text
central claim
hero evidence
supporting evidence
replication evidence
boundary or limitation evidence
reading order
reference figure, if any
```

The primary visual anchor is the evidence that most directly carries the claim, uses the strongest biological unit, compresses meaningful dimensions, and cannot be replaced by a nearby support panel. Use one hero by default, but allow a **dual-anchor** structure when two complementary views are jointly necessary and visually co-equal, as in the approved high-impact omics benchmark. Do not extend this into three or more equal heroes.

## Composition

- Use asymmetric layouts when evidence roles or information density differ.
- Give image plates, cohort-wide matrices, or central multidimensional evidence enough area to remain legible.
- Keep two-group summaries and simple effect plots compact.
- Do not enlarge weak evidence to complete a grid.
- Do not force the composition to fill the selected authoring or delivery canvas. Unused area is acceptable when it supports the reading rhythm.
- Crop the delivery canvas after layout without rescaling the content.

## Visual hierarchy

- Use area, position, contrast, and label weight consistently.
- Reserve the strongest colour for one focal result or direction.
- De-emphasize support evidence with lower saturation and lighter strokes, not by deleting it.
- Prefer direct labels when they reduce eye travel and do not create collisions.
- Share legends across panels or remove them when direct labels are clearer.

## Information density

- Retain high information density when the reading path is clear.
- Remove repeated titles, repeated legends, decorative icons, redundant `ns` labels, and prose-like method boxes.
- Use whitespace to separate evidence blocks, not to manufacture balance.
- If the figure requires zooming at its declared viewing size, revise the layout or split the figure.

## Reference-first use

When the user provides a high-level reference figure:

1. identify its reading order, hero, area ratios, direct-label strategy, and colour hierarchy;
2. identify which visual grammar is supported by the user's data;
3. adapt the hierarchy without copying the reference's unique graphic expression;
4. compare a concise candidate with an art-directed candidate;
5. choose using scientific fit, legibility, and area economy.
